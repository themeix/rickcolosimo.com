I’ve written before about the growing inconsistency between the actual [job market for lawyers](/cutting-starting-salaries-will-hurt-young-lawyers/) and the jobs that law schools tell prospective students about.

Here’s a recent Above the Law [post on a job](http://abovethelaw.com/2010/05/10-an-hour-for-a-j-d/) that really doesn’t appear all that worthwhile, really.

This is a downgrade from the [$12 job](http://abovethelaw.com/2010/03/did-you-go-to-law-school-to-earn-12-an-hour/) they link to at the bottom of the post, but it’s a step up from the California job FOR AN ADMITTED ATTORNEY that paid so little it [violated minimum wage laws](http://abovethelaw.com/2010/04/a-round-up-of-terrible-10hour-lawyer-jobs-in-california/#more-12271).

Wowza. I suppose my economist friend would say this tells us something about the shape of the demand curve, right? But what, exactly?

Is this a supply problem, of there actually being too many lawyers, or is it a matter of there being such a demand for work that is legal in nature but doesn’t merit paying high costs for that work?
